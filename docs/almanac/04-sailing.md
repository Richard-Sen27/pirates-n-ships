A ship is only as good as the hand who sails her. Learn the wind, learn your canvas, and learn to keep the bow where you want it. The rest is patience.

## The wind
Every sea has one wind, the same for every soul on it. It blows from one quarter at a time, between a light air and a fresh gale, and it veers and freshens slowly through the day.

Rain stiffens it by half again, and a thunderstorm more than doubles it and brings sudden gusts.

You can read the wind without instruments. Out on the water, thin white streaks drift past from where it comes, more of them and faster the harder it blows. Flags stream downwind, and set sails belly out to leeward.

> Aboard, the compass rose at the bottom of your view carries a pale arrow on the side the wind comes from. The longer the arrow, the stronger the wind; it glows amber in a gust.

## Square sails
illustration: yard
show_me: sails_and_winch | Show me: rigging a sail and working the winch
A square sail hangs between two {item:pirates_n_ships:yard}**yards**. A yard is a row of yard blocks laid end to end across the mast; its middle block marks the mast.

1. Set the upper yard across the mast.
2. Set a second yard of the same direction 2 to 8 blocks straight below it. Between the two middle blocks there may be only air or mast (logs, fences).
3. The pair is now one sail. The cloth is drawn between them; you place no canvas blocks.

The sail's area is the yards' mean length times the drop between them: two yards 5 wide and 5 apart give 25. A yard may be up to 15 blocks long. Three yards on one mast make two sails.

A square sail loves a wind from astern and is useless within 45° of the wind.

> Click a yard that heads no sail and it tells you what is wrong with the rig.

## Fore-and-aft sails
A triangular sail is rigged with {item:pirates_n_ships:rope}**rope** and three {item:pirates_n_ships:cleat}**cleats**.

1. Put one cleat high on the side of the mast: the head.
2. Put one on the deck or a bowsprit further forward, at least 2 blocks lower: the tack.
3. Put one straight below the head: the clew.
4. Use a rope on the head cleat, then on the tack cleat, no more than 16 blocks away. The rope is spent and a stay runs between them.

The cloth fills the triangle between the stay and the clew. Its area is half of head-to-tack times head-to-clew. A fore-and-aft sail is the one for a wind on the beam: it still drives at 45° off the wind.

Click the head cleat with an empty hand to work its trim. Break the clew and the cloth goes but the stay stays; break the tack and you get your rope back.

> A rope between two cleats or mooring rings that is no stay becomes a rope line: a sagging rope for a rail or a rigging. Each cleat or ring holds up to four.

## Trimming the canvas
illustration: sail_winch
Every sail has three trims: furled, half and full. Half sail draws half the force of full.

- Use the {item:pirates_n_ships:sail_winch}**sail winch** on deck to work **every** sail of the ship together: furled, then half, then full, then furled again.
- Click an upper yard, or the head cleat of a triangular sail, with an empty hand to work that one sail alone.

The force grows with the wind you feel on board, the area of the canvas and its trim. No sail drives within 30° of the wind, and a ship running before the wind never outruns it. Sails work only on a ship afloat.

You need not brace the yards: the crew is assumed to trim every sail as well as it can be trimmed. Watch the cloth instead. A sail that draws bellies out to leeward; a sail that shakes and hangs slack is in the no-go zone, and you must bear away.

> Your hull holds you on course. A ship slides ahead far more easily than sideways, so a beam wind drives her forward instead of pushing her to leeward. No keel block is needed.

## The helm
show_me: steering | Show me: steering at the helm
Hold right-click on the {item:pirates_n_ships:helm}**helm** of an assembled ship to take the wheel. Then move the mouse, or hold A and D, to turn it: right or D puts her to starboard, left or A to port. The wheel turns three quarters of a turn each way from midships.

The line above your hotbar shows the rudder angle, up to 35° either side. Let go and the wheel stays where you left it.

The rudder bites only while the ship moves through the water, and it works the other way round when she makes sternway. A slow ship turns on the same circle as a fast one; she just takes longer to sail it.

> If your server has turned off steering by dragging, click the wheel instead: its right third one step to starboard, its left third to port, the middle back to midships.

## Keeping her upright
Wind on the side of a sail heels the ship over, and the hull rights itself. A wide, shallow hull stands stiffest. As the heel nears 25°, the sails spill their wind, so no storm will lay a sound ship on her side under canvas alone.

The shipwright ballasts every ship as you built her: a tall stern castle or a heavy gun deck on one side does not tip her. What you load later does. Cargo stowed to one side and water in the hold make her list; pump her dry and trim the load, as [[pirates_n_ships:trade|Cargo and trade]] explains.

> A long, wide hull stands steadier than a short, narrow one. Build her with some beam if you mean to carry a press of sail.

## Anchor and capstan
illustration: capstan
show_me: anchor_and_capstan | Show me: dropping and weighing anchor
The {item:pirates_n_ships:capstan}**capstan** works the anchor. The anchor hangs outside the hull on the capstan's side, just below the deck.

- Use the capstan to let go. The anchor sinks fast, with the chain rattling, and lands a few blocks astern if you still had way on.
- Once it bites, the chain pulls her up gently at the bow. She swings round toward the capstan's side and lies head to her chain.
- She counts as anchored once the anchor holds and she has stopped.
- Use the capstan again to weigh: the chain comes in at a steady pace and drags the anchor up to the hull. Use it again midway to let go once more.

The chain is 32 blocks long. Over deeper water the capstan refuses to let go, for the anchor would find no bottom.

>! A ship pulling harder than her anchor holds drags it over the seabed. Furl before you anchor.

## Mooring and lines
show_me: mooring | Show me: mooring alongside
There are no mooring lines to belay at a quay: a ship tied up is simply a ship lying still at the berth. Bring her in slowly, drop the anchor, and she is moored. A harbour counts your ship as moored at its port once she lies within about 32 blocks of it.

{item:pirates_n_ships:mooring_ring}**Mooring rings** and **cleats** serve the grappling hook. A hook flying close to a ring catches on it, and a hook held by a ring holds twice as far before it tears loose. With your hook set in another ship, use a ring or a cleat on your own ship to make the rope fast there; the ships keep hauling together and you can walk away. A ring on land holds a hooked ship the same way. The [[pirates_n_ships:combat|Weapons and combat]] chapter tells the rest of the hook.

## Waves and weather
The sea follows the weather. In fair weather there is a low swell or a light chop; in rain the sea runs rough; in a thunderstorm the crests stand two blocks and more above the still water. The change comes over about a minute.

Waves come in sets: a run of bigger seas, then a calmer spell. Your ship rises on the crests and sinks in the troughs, a small boat far more than a big one, and in heavy weather the bow throws spray.

A rowing boat rides the swell too, climbing the same crests out on open water, and anything you throw overboard bobs along with it; on lakes, rivers and in the shallows by the shore the water lies still as ever.

>! In rough seas every open hatch or low rim near the waterline takes water at the crests. Close your hatches and keep a pump manned before a storm.

A moving ship leaves a wake: a bow wave spreading from the stem and a trail of foam astern that fades over some seconds.

## Ratlines
illustration: ratlines
{item:pirates_n_ships:ratlines}**Ratlines** are rope nets for climbing aloft, to a crow's nest or a yard.

- Click the side of a mast to hang a net on it like a ladder.
- Click the top of the deck or the gunwale to lay a sloped net instead. It rises at 45° the way you face, so stand at the gunwale looking at the mast.
- Click a ratline you placed to add the next one at the end of its run, straight up a hanging net or up and forward along a sloped one.
- Sneak to place against the clicked side instead.

Climb a hanging net like a ladder; walk up a sloped one. A net needs something to rest on: break one and the nets that hung from it fall too. A sloped run may end on a yard right beside the mast, and from there you climb straight on up the hanging net above.

## Ratline nets
{item:pirates_n_ships:ratline_net}A **ratline net** runs from a **chainplate** at the rail to a **mast hoop** up the mast, at any slope from 30° to straight up. Put the chainplate on top of the rail or on its side and the hoop on the mast, then use the net on one and then on the other. The line between their rings must be clear of blocks and at most 12 blocks long; you are told what is wrong otherwise. Walk into the net and hold forward or jump to climb it along its line; sneak to hold on. Break the net, either fitting or put a block into the net and the whole net comes down and drops at the first fitting; the fittings stay. The net sails with the ship.

## The ship's bell
illustration: ships_bell
show_me: ships_bell | Show me: the ship's bell
The {item:pirates_n_ships:ships_bell}**ship's bell** sits on a post or a wall. Right-click it and it swings and rings.

It is a bell and nothing more: it does not call your crew. But mark it when you lie in a harbour: when raiders come, every bell in the settlement rings the alarm.

## The crow's nest
illustration: crows_nest
show_me: crows_nest | Show me: keeping the lookout
The {item:pirates_n_ships:crows_nest}**crow's nest** is a barrel for the masthead. Place it on a log, a fence, a wall or any block with a solid top; if that block goes, the nest falls. Climb up the mast and step in over the rim, and sneak to stay put.

Whoever stands in the nest keeps the lookout, you or a hand you post there with the whistle. Every few seconds the lookout sweeps 160 blocks around the ship and calls what is new to everyone aboard:

- **Sail ho!** A ship, by the flag she flies: merchant, navy, pirate, a wreck, or just a ship.
- **Land ho!** The nearest coast, once per stretch of shore.
- Sharks, and the kraken.

Bearings are called in points of the compass from the bow: dead ahead, two points off the starboard bow, on the beam, abaft the beam, dead astern. A full circle is 32 points. The lookout calls each sighting once and remembers it a while after it drops from view.

## The ship's panels
While you are aboard, two small panels watch over her.

- **The compass,** bottom left: a ship-shaped needle points along your heading, the wind arrow sits outside the rose, and below it you read the speed in knots and the rudder angle.
- **The hull strip,** bottom right: one cell per compartment from bow to stern, filling blue as water comes in, with a red mark at a breach and a pump sign where a pump works.

They stay while you walk the deck, climb the rigging or stand at the helm, and go a moment after you leave her. Her name, load and crew are on the ship screen at the helm; see [[pirates_n_ships:ships|Ships]].

## Music at sea
Aboard, the next tune is a sea shanty; at sea but not aboard, an air of wind and water. A tune is never cut off, and a few minutes of quiet pass between them.
